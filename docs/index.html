How this dashboard works

This dashboard watches the weather at the 36 Philippine airports served by Cebu Pacific and Cebgo, plus earthquakes and typhoons nearby. It shows which airports need attention, why, and how reliable each reading is.

1. How to read and use it

NormalNo bad weather expected today. Shown on the map only.
AdvisoryBad weather is expected later today. Plan around it.
WarningBad weather is expected within 1 hour, or is already happening. As a precaution, be ready to pause ramp work and stay careful.
DangerA thunderstorm is at the airport right now. Work at the ramp with extra care. Safety is the priority at all times.

The screen

  • Level counts. The four large numbers (Danger, Warning, Advisory, Normal) show how many airports are at each level. Press one to show only that level on the map and in the list; press it again to go back. On a phone these are the five tiles under the alerts.
  • Today and Tomorrow. At the top. Switches the list between today's alerts and airports with bad weather forecast for tomorrow.
  • Region and Find an airport. Region narrows everything to Luzon, Visayas or Mindanao. Find an airport jumps straight to one airport.
  • Map. The map fills the left side of the screen. The rain timeline and the Auto-show switch are just under it. Each dot is an airport, coloured by its level. A flashing dot means a thunderstorm there now. A dashed outline means the reading is an Estimate. On wide screens the neighbouring countries are drawn around the Philippines so the rain forecast can be seen coming in; they are a picture only and cannot be selected. Triangles are volcanoes: filled means an eruption or a raised alert level, and a brown dashed area is an official ash cloud warning. Purple rings are earthquakes: larger means stronger, faded means more than 24 hours old. To keep the map clear, airport names show for Danger airports, for the airport you select, and when you zoom in; hover or tap any dot to see its name. The Layers button turns rain, earthquakes, all volcanoes and all airport names on or off, and remembers your choice on that device.
  • Moving around the map. Use the + and − buttons, the mouse wheel, pinch, or double-tap to zoom, and drag to move. The round arrow under them goes back to the whole map and clears the selection.
  • Airport list. On the right, under the key figures. Lists the airports at the chosen level with what to expect and when. Press Details, or anywhere on a row, to open that airport.
  • Key figures. The tiles at the top right: airports needing attention, thunderstorms at an airport now, airports with an official weather report, and bad weather forecast for tomorrow. The smaller tiles show earthquake alerts, volcano alerts and typhoon; one turns red when it has an alert, and pressing it opens that list.
  • Status panel. Beside or under the airport list. Details shows the level counts, and Hazards holds the full lists for earthquakes, volcanoes and typhoon; a section opens by itself when it has an alert. Windy map opens a more detailed rain map from Windy.com.
  • Details window. Selecting an airport, earthquake or volcano opens its details in a window on the right. Press the X to close it.
  • Alerts over the map. Earthquake, volcano and Danger alerts appear as coloured cards at the top of the map. Press Details on a card to open it. If there are more than three, press "more" to see the rest.
  • Bell and question mark. At the top right. The bell turns the alert chime on or off, and the question mark opens this guide.
  • On a phone. The map, the airport list and the details become three tabs.

An airport's details

  • Right now and Rest of today. What the latest report or estimate says, with clock times.
  • Rain in the next 12 hours. For each hour: the chance of rain as a percentage, a bar and a word for how heavy (Dry, Light, Moderate, Heavy), and the amount in millimetres. A lightning mark shows hours when the official airport forecast says a thunderstorm is possible.
  • Tomorrow. What the official forecast and the estimate say about tomorrow.
  • Next 7 days. For each day: the chance of rain, a day picture and a night picture (a lightning bolt means a thunderstorm is forecast), and the high and low temperature. It is for planning and is less certain the further ahead.
  • What to do. The action that goes with the alert level.
  • Confidence, Updated and Source. How far to trust the reading and why, when it was taken, and who supplied it.

Rain on the map

  • Rain timeline. The map plays the forecast rain for the next 24 hours by itself. Use Stop and Play, drag the slider to any hour, or press Hide rain.
  • Lightning marks and dashed areas. These come only from official sources: thunderstorm area warnings and official airport forecasts. Rain with no lightning mark does not mean no thunder.
  • It is a picture of the forecast. It plays no part in the alert levels, which take priority when the two differ. The Windy map is a different forecast again, so it can differ from both.

Alerts and what the page does by itself

  • Alert bar. A coloured bar appears under the title when an airport is at Danger, a strong earthquake has happened, a tsunami is possible, or there is a volcano alert. The browser tab shows a warning sign as well. Tap a name or Details in the bar to go to it. Each alert is one short line; press Details for the full information. When there are more than three, the rest fold under a "more" link.
  • Sound. Press the bell at the top to turn on a soft two-note chime, played once for each new alert. It is quiet on purpose: it asks for attention without causing alarm. Browsers only allow sound after someone has clicked on the page.
  • Your nearest airport. When the page is opened or reloaded, the browser asks once for your location. If you allow it, the map first shows the airport nearest to you. Your location is used only inside your browser; it is not sent anywhere or stored.
  • Auto-show alerts. When there is an earthquake alert or an airport at Danger and nobody is using the screen, the map shows each one in turn: earthquakes first, then volcano alerts, then Danger airports. It zooms in, shows the details, then returns to the whole map and repeats. A new earthquake alert is shown straight away. Earthquakes stay in the rotation for 6 hours. Touching the screen pauses this for a minute. A small label on the map lets you turn it off.
  • The times at the top. "Checked" is when the data was last collected. "Next update" is that time plus the average gap between recent updates; it is an estimate, because the schedule does not run at exact times. An open page looks for new data by itself, every minute once an update is due, so you never need to reload.
  • Yellow notice. Appears when a backup source is in use or the data is more than 60 minutes old.

2. Where the data comes from

Each kind of data has a first-choice source and, where one exists, a backup. If the first choice cannot be reached, the dashboard switches to the backup by itself. Section 4 below shows which source supplied the data on screen right now.

Official airport reports and forecastsIssued for Philippine airports by PAGASA, the country's designated weather authority for aviation, and shared worldwide. This is the most reliable weather source. Read from aviationweather.gov, the United States government aviation weather service. Backup: the NOAA data server, which carries the same reports.
EstimatesFor airports with no official report, a computer weather forecast for that exact location. These are marked Estimate. They show rain and wind only and cannot confirm a thunderstorm. First choice: MET Norway, the Norwegian national weather institute. Backup: Open-Meteo.
Chance of rain and the 7-day outlookFrom Open-Meteo, a computer forecast. Used only for the hourly rain chance and the Next 7 days list. It never affects an alert level, and if it cannot be reached those two items are simply left out.
Storm and typhoon warningsFirst choice: official aviation storm warnings from aviationweather.gov, which show where a tropical cyclone is, how it is moving and where it will be in the next 6 hours, and which areas have thunderstorm warnings. Backup: GDACS, the United Nations and European Union disaster alert system, which shows position only.
EarthquakesFirst choice: PHIVOLCS, the Philippine government agency with the most earthquake sensors inside the country. Backups: USGS (United States Geological Survey), then EMSC (European seismological centre). Agencies can report slightly different magnitudes for the same earthquake.
VolcanoesAsh clouds and eruptions: official aviation volcanic ash warnings (SIGMET) from aviationweather.gov, the same feed used for typhoons. Volcano Alert Levels: PHIVOLCS volcano bulletins; if they cannot be read automatically, the level shows as not available or uses figures entered by the IT Department.
Rain on the mapA MET Norway forecast sampled on a grid of points across the region, rebuilt every 3 hours. The optional detailed map is provided by Windy.com.

3. The rules it follows

  • Danger is only given when an official airport report says a thunderstorm is at the airport now. An Estimate can never be Danger.
  • Bad weather in an official report or forecast means a thunderstorm, rain or rain showers, or strong winds.
  • "Possible at times" is how the official airport forecast describes weather that may come and go during a period, usually for less than an hour at a stretch. It can happen; it is not certain. "Expected" means the forecast is firm.
  • Bad weather in an Estimate means 2.5 mm or more of rain in an hour, or winds of 39 km/h or more.
  • How heavy. Light is under 2.5 mm of rain in an hour, Moderate is 2.5 mm or more, Heavy is 7.6 mm or more.
  • Area warnings. An airport inside an official thunderstorm or tropical cyclone area warning is raised to Warning.
  • Earthquakes shown are magnitude 4.5 or stronger in the Philippine area over the past 7 days.
  • Aftershocks. Smaller earthquakes (below magnitude 5.0) within 100 km of a main earthquake of magnitude 5.0 or stronger, in the 72 hours after it, are grouped with it. Each is a small purple dot on the map, and the main earthquake's details list them together.
  • Why magnitudes differ. Each agency measures with its own instruments and method, so PHIVOLCS, USGS and phone alerts can show different figures for the same earthquake, and first figures are often revised. The dashboard shows the PHIVOLCS figure, shows the USGS figure beside it for strong earthquakes, and uses the more cautious of the two positions when deciding whether an airport is within 100 km.
  • Early notice. The data is collected about every 20 minutes. In between, an open page asks USGS once a minute. An earthquake that meets the alert rules is shown at once as an early notice, then replaced by the confirmed figures at the next update. USGS usually publishes within about 10 to 20 minutes of an earthquake, so no source is instant.
  • Earthquake alerts cover the last 24 hours: magnitude 5.0 or stronger within 100 km of an airport, or magnitude 6.0 or stronger anywhere in the Philippine area.
  • Possible tsunami is shown for an earthquake of magnitude 6.5 or stronger no deeper than 70 km, or one that USGS has flagged for tsunami information. It is a prompt to check PHIVOLCS bulletins, not an official tsunami warning.
  • Volcanoes work like earthquakes: they raise their own alerts and never change an airport's weather level. A volcano counts as near an airport within 150 km. There is no official standard distance; 150 km covers about half of all past airport disruptions worldwide (US Geological Survey study).
  • Volcanic ash alert. Raised when an official aviation ash warning area covers an airport, at any distance from the volcano. A few millimetres of ash on a runway is enough to close an airport.
  • Eruption alert. Raised when an official ash warning reports an eruption at a volcano within 150 km of an airport.
  • Volcano Alert Level is the PHIVOLCS scale: 0 normal, 1 low-level unrest, 2 increasing unrest, 3 high unrest (eruption possible within weeks), 4 hazardous eruption imminent, 5 hazardous eruption in progress. Level 3 or higher within 150 km of an airport raises an alert. Ash can affect flights at any level, which is why ash warnings are checked separately.
  • Which source wins. When sources differ, the dashboard uses the more reliable one, in this order: official airport report, official airport forecast, official area warning, then Estimate.
  • Backups. If a first-choice source cannot be reached, its backup is used and a yellow notice says so. Idle backups are tested every 6 hours.

5. What it cannot do

  • It supports decisions. It does not replace official bulletins, official tsunami warnings, or the judgement of staff at the airport.
  • It does not detect lightning. Thunderstorm information comes from official reports, forecasts and area warnings.
  • Estimates show rain and wind only and cannot confirm a thunderstorm.
  • Earthquakes cannot be predicted. Only those that have already happened are shown.
  • Forecasts for tomorrow and the days ahead are less certain than today.
  • PAGASA's public typhoon bulletins are not read directly, because they are not published as data. Confirm typhoon decisions against PAGASA.
